The app and its goals

Shura wa Istehsan is an open archive of hadith across four traditions: Sunni, Imami, Ibadi and Zaydi. Most hadith sites show the text and a grade. This one shows how a report reached us: who narrated it from whom, what is known about each narrator, when and where they lived, and what scholars have said about the report.

It is built for serious readers who do not have a seminary library: practising Muslims who want to understand why a report is graded as it is, self-taught students, teachers who need sourced material, and researchers.

What a reader can do

  • Read a report in Arabic with a translation, its source work and its place in that work.
  • Follow its chain of transmission, narrator by narrator, including reports that reach us through several branches.
  • Open a narrator to see teachers and students, assessments by critics, life dates, and events during their lifetime.
  • See gradings as each tradition gives them. A Sunni verdict and a Zaydi verdict on the same narrator sit side by side; neither overrides the other.
  • Read commentary and historical context linked to a report or a narrator.
  • Compare with other scriptures. Parallels in Jewish, Christian and other sources are catalogued with the classical Isra'iliyyat classification.

Goals

  • Curation, not broadcast. The archive presents evidence and attributed scholarship. It does not issue rulings, and no computed score is presented as a verdict.
  • Anti-sectarian. Every tradition's sources and grading schemes are first-class. Nothing defaults to one tradition.
  • Cumulative scholarship. In the classical manner of commentary upon commentary, contemporary scholars add attributed, reviewed work that stays linked to what it builds on.
  • Open. The code is open source and the published data is meant to be exportable with attribution.
  • Honest about gaps. What is unknown is shown as unknown.

Rules the schema enforces

RuleHow the schema holds it
The Arabic source is never alteredStored byte for byte with a hash. Translations record the hash of the Arabic they rendered, so a translation cannot silently drift from its source.
A name is not a personEach name in a chain is stored as written. It links to a narrator identity only when that identity is established; otherwise it is displayed as text.
Grades belong to a traditionEvery assessment carries its tradition and grading scheme. No narrator has a single reliability value. "No evaluation exists" is the absence of a row.
Disagreement is keptCompeting claims about a date or a biography are separate rows, each with its citation. None is collapsed into one answer.
Reloading data never erases scholarshipTables are written either by the data pipeline or by people through the app, never both. A reload updates pipeline rows by permanent ID and leaves human work alone.
Nothing is deletedRows are retired with a timestamp and an audit record.
Unreviewed machine output stays separateMachine translations and proposed name matches live in candidate tables until a person reviews them.
Every change has an authorAn audit table records who changed what, with before and after.

Contribution

planned6 tables

Not part of the 48. These ship with the contribution features, and replace the small fixed list of named curators that access control starts with.

What each table holds
  • contributors A person's scholarly profile: name, institution, areas of expertise, and role (reader, contributor, reviewer, editor, admin).
  • contributor_endorsements One scholar vouching for another in a named field. The chain of endorsements is public, like a chain of scholarly authority.
  • submissions One proposed contribution: its type, what it is about, its content, and its status from draft to published or rejected.
  • submission_reviews A reviewer's verdict and notes on a submission.
  • submission_comments The discussion between reviewer and contributor.
  • collection_imports A bulk upload: file hash, validation report, preview counts, and the dataset version it became.

How publishing works. A submission holds proposed content. When an editor publishes it, the content is written into the table where that kind of scholarship already lives (a commentary into commentaries, a narrator evaluation into narrator_assessments, a correction as a cited claim), and the audit record names the contributor and the submission. Readers query the same tables either way.

Where the plan and the current design differ. The plan's "graph-native" principle is kept in substance: every relationship is its own table with its own attributes. Its data exports as a Neo4j dump become a Postgres dump plus CSV. Its figures (about 34,500 hadiths from six collections) predate the four-tradition corpus. And where older product notes describe an automatically calculated grade, the design shows chain facts and attributed verdicts only.

Dataset limitations

Figures are from the audit of 21 July 2026 unless stated. They describe the data as it stands, before any gap-closing work.

Coverage

LimitationExtent
The corpus is uneven across traditionsSunni 64,356 · Imami 33,225 · Ibadi 1,004 · Zaydi 698
Major works are absentImami: Tahdhib al-Ahkam, al-Istibsar, Bihar al-Anwar, Wasa'il al-Shi'a. Sunni: the works after the six books (al-Bayhaqi, Ibn Hibban, Ibn Khuzayma, al-Tabarani, al-Hakim, the two Musannafs). Zaydi and Ibadi: most works beyond one or two each.
No commentariesThe commentary tables are empty. None of the classical commentaries is ingested.
Historical context is a starter set54 events and 32 locations, almost all single-year points, with no dynasties or eras.
